Zomato is one of the most successful startups in India. And I always find myself fascinated by the engineering behind it. When something works this seamlessly, no friction, no second-guessing, just order and wait, it means a great team of engineers has spent days and nights solving problems you never even see.

Problems. Yes. And one that I keep coming back to: what if no rider has accepted my order yet? How does a system that looks completely calm on the outside manage to panic on the inside, because it made me a promise, and it simply cannot walk away from that promise. It must struggle. Hard enough to exhaust every available option before it considers cancelling my order and issuing a refund.

It sounds simple on the surface. But once you tear apart each engineering layer, you'll find one of the most thoughtful and quietly complex parts of Zomato's entire backend.

---

So let's dive in.

But before we do, quick introduction: I'm Himanshu Saini, a 23 year old nerd working as Tech Lead at Hobfit. You'll find us on Shark Tank Season 5 soon enough.

Now, before things go critical, and by critical I mean critical enough that the system has to press the RED Refund Button, let's look at what normal looks like first.

Say today is the 1st of the month. Salary just credited. I open Zomato, spot my favorite Dosa at 66% off, delivery time 30 minutes, every flag is green. Green enough that I finally enter my UPI pin, hand over my money to the one app that reliably brings food to my doorstep, and go back to Instagram Reels. Dosa is coming in 30 minutes anyway.

Perfect setup. Even better if it's a weekend and I'm already in bed.

But let's keep things normal for now. What happens next? Obvious answer: Zomato starts looking for nearby riders.

**T=0.** Zomato queries all riders within a 3 km radius of the restaurant. Each rider's app polls the server every 2 to 3 seconds, writing their updated GPS coordinates to Redis using `GEOADD`:

```
GEOADD riders 77.2090 28.6139 "rider:456"
```

The dispatch service then runs a single `GEOSEARCH` command against that Redis instance:

```
GEOSEARCH riders FROMLONLAT 77.2090 28.6139
  BYRADIUS 3 km ASC
  COUNT 50
  ASC
```

This returns a ranked list of nearby available riders, sorted by distance, in under a millisecond. Yes, Redis. Not PostgreSQL. Storing and querying real-time GPS coordinates for 300,000 active riders in a relational database would set the server on fire. Fire hot enough that the engineering team wouldn't have time to dial 101 before the whole thing melted.

---

Zomato sends a push notification with the order details and the delivery fee offered, to every rider that ranked in that query. Actually, let me take that back, not every rider. Only riders who were online, not currently on a delivery, or close enough to finishing a nearby drop that batching makes sense.

Normal condition? A rider accepts, starts heading to the restaurant, your Dosa is on its way. Simple. Beautiful. Done.

But let's say your planetary position gets a little misaligned.

Misaligned enough that every single rider who just got notified about your order suddenly received a call from their wife, logged off the app, and headed home instead of the restaurant.

Now what?

Has anyone even thought about this case? Was the QA engineer at Zomato paid enough to think about this? Of course they were. I can almost guarantee that somewhere in Zomato's codebase there is a test case with a comment that reads:

```
# this case handles the scenario where every notified rider
# has received a call from their wife
```

**T=30s.** No rider has accepted. The system notices. Zomato doesn't have access to your birth chart to check your planetary position, if they did, they probably wouldn't have let you place the order in the first place. Instead, the dispatch service runs the exact same `GEOSEARCH` query again, same radius, same restaurant coordinates, because new riders may have entered the 3 km zone in the last 30 seconds:

```
GEOSEARCH riders FROMLONLAT 77.2090 28.6139
  BYRADIUS 3 km ASC
  COUNT 50
  ASC
```

A rider finishing a nearby delivery, someone who just came online. Zomato notifies them.

But God has decided today is not your Dosa day.

The planets misalign further. This batch of riders suddenly remembers they have a wedding to attend. And unlike your Dosa, the wedding has free food. So instead of heading to the restaurant to pick up your order, they headed straight to Vatika.

Who cares about your Dosa when there's free biryani at a wedding.

---

But Zomato will keep its promise against all odds. Or at least try its best. Even if it means bribing God.

**T=60s.** No acceptance yet. This is where panic quietly kicks in on the backend. That Dosa means just a Dosa with Sambhar and Nariyal Chutney to you. To Zomato it means their existence. They must get it to your stomach.

The dispatch service updates the `GEOSEARCH` query, radius now 5 km:

```
GEOSEARCH riders FROMLONLAT 77.2090 28.6139
  BYRADIUS 5 km ASC
  COUNT 50
  ASC
```

You got 66% off on the order. Zomato is fine with that. But now they are also going to incentivise riders to pick this order on priority, even if it means losing their own margin on the delivery. That is why I love Zomato.

But God has decided. No Dosa for you today.

Just as a delivery partner spotted the incentive and was about to accept, his phone battery died. Not because it wasn't charged. Because you carry enough bad luck that it channelled directly into his device.

While riders were enjoying the wedding and shopping for their wives, your Dosa sat at the restaurant counter getting cold, waiting to be picked up by someone, anyone.

**T=90s.** Zomato is now putting everything on the table. This is the gamble. `GEOSEARCH` expands again to 8 km, incentive increases again:

```
GEOSEARCH riders FROMLONLAT 77.2090 28.6139
  BYRADIUS 8 km ASC
  COUNT 50
  ASC
```

One and a half minutes have passed. No rider found. But Zomato is not like your girlfriend who breaks promises. Zomato will keep its promise until its last breath.

---

But here is where Zomato does something most engineers don't think about when designing dispatch systems. Something that doesn't make it into most system design interviews. Something quietly brilliant.

While the `GEOSEARCH` has been expanding its radius looking for idle riders, there is another type of rider the system has been watching. A rider who is currently mid-delivery. Already on the road. Already moving. Heading to drop off someone else's order somewhere close to your restaurant.

Zomato calls this a batching candidate.

The dispatch service knows where every active rider is heading and when they are expected to finish their current drop. If a rider's drop location is within a reasonable distance of your restaurant, and their estimated finish time aligns with when your Dosa will be ready, the system can pre-assign them to your order before they even finish their current delivery.

The rider gets a notification mid-ride. Something like: "New order nearby after your current drop." They accept while still delivering someone else's biryani. The moment they hand off that order, they turn directly toward your restaurant without ever going idle. No gap. No wasted movement.

For you, this means the ETA on your screen quietly updates. Not because something went wrong. Because Zomato just found a rider in a way the naive dispatch model never considered.

This is the part that separates a good dispatch system from a great one. A simple system looks for idle riders. Zomato's system looks at the entire fleet, idle and active, and asks which rider, accounting for their current trajectory, their expected finish time, and the distance to your restaurant, can get to you the fastest.

The `GEOSEARCH` finds who is nearby. The batching logic finds who is useful.

Those are two different questions. And asking both of them is what keeps your Dosa alive when the planets are misaligned.

---

But every system has a limit. Even Zomato.

There is a hard truth sitting quietly behind all this escalation logic. Your Dosa has been sitting on the restaurant counter for a while now. The Sambhar is cooling. The Nariyal Chutney is starting to dry at the edges. The restaurant staff have glanced at it three times already wondering when someone is going to pick it up.

At some point, even if a rider accepts right now, the promise is already broken. Not the delivery promise. The food quality promise. The 30 minutes you were shown at checkout wasn't just a delivery time. It was a freshness contract.

This is where the order state machine becomes the most important piece of engineering in the entire system.

Every Zomato order lives in a state machine. A finite set of states it can be in at any point in time, with strict rules about which transitions are allowed. The happy path states are the ones everyone knows: `ORDER_PLACED`, `RESTAURANT_ACCEPTED`, `RIDER_ASSIGNED`, `ORDER_PICKED_UP`, `ORDER_DELIVERED`.

But the failure states are where the real engineering lives.

When no rider accepts within the normal window, the order transitions to `AWAITING_ASSIGNMENT`. The escalation loop runs. Radius expands. Incentives increase. Batching candidates are evaluated. The system is doing everything it can.

But the order state machine has a timer running in parallel with all of this. A hard timeout. Not on the assignment attempt, but on the order age. The moment your food has been sitting at the restaurant beyond a threshold, somewhere around 20 to 25 minutes depending on the food type and restaurant, the system knows that fulfilling this order is no longer in your interest even if it is technically possible.

A rider accepting at this point doesn't fix the problem. It makes it worse. You wait another 20 minutes for delivery, receive cold food, leave a one star review, call customer support, and never order from that restaurant again. Zomato loses more from fulfilling this order badly than from cancelling it cleanly.

So the state machine transitions to `ASSIGNMENT_FAILED`.

And this transition is irreversible. Once an order enters `ASSIGNMENT_FAILED`, no amount of radius expansion or incentive increase can pull it back. The escalation loop terminates. The order is done.

What happens next is a coordinated sequence that fires simultaneously across multiple services. The restaurant gets notified to stop holding the food. The payment service triggers a full refund to your original payment method, usually within seconds for UPI. The notification service sends you a cancellation message. And somewhere in Zomato's internal ops dashboard, a flag gets raised if this failure happened in a geography where it is happening repeatedly, because repeated `ASSIGNMENT_FAILED` transitions in the same zone at the same time of day are not bad luck. They are a supply problem. And supply problems need human attention, not just a better algorithm.

The RED Refund Button, as it turns out, is not a single button. It is a coordinated state transition that touches the restaurant, the payment system, the customer, and the ops team in one atomic sequence.

---

Now let's talk about what you actually saw on your screen during all of this.

"Finding a delivery partner."

That's it. That's all Zomato showed you. A calm, reassuring message with a gentle animation. No "radius expanded to 8 km." No "incentive increased by ₹30." No "batching candidate evaluated." No "your order is in `AWAITING_ASSIGNMENT` state and the escalation loop is on its fourth iteration."

Just "Finding a delivery partner."

This is not an accident. This is one of the most deliberate product decisions in the entire system.

Zomato knows something important about human psychology. The moment you see "No riders available" you cancel. Not because the situation is hopeless, because the system is actively working to fix it, but because uncertainty is more uncomfortable than loss. A refund feels cleaner than waiting for something that might not come. So Zomato never shows you the uncertainty. They show you progress, or at least the appearance of progress, while the backend does the hard work.

Your ETA quietly updated from 30 minutes to 42 minutes somewhere around T=60s. You probably noticed. You probably assumed traffic. You didn't know it was because the system just expanded the search radius to 5 km and the nearest available rider is now further away. Zomato didn't lie to you. They just didn't explain themselves. The ETA update was honest. The silence around why it updated was a product decision.

This is a pattern that shows up in every great consumer product at scale. The backend complexity is real and it is enormous. But the user experience sits on top of it like a still lake on top of a storm drain. Everything turbulent below. Everything calm above.

The "Finding a delivery partner" message is doing more emotional work than any other string in Zomato's entire frontend codebase. It is buying the dispatch system time. Every second you spend reading that message calmly is a second the escalation loop gets to run another iteration, expand another radius, evaluate another batching candidate, notify another rider.

The engineering keeps the promise. The UX protects the trust while the engineering does its job.

And if the system ultimately fails, if the order hits `ASSIGNMENT_FAILED` and the RED Refund Button fires, that is the first and only moment Zomato breaks the calm surface and tells you something went wrong. Not with a technical explanation. Just a clean cancellation notification and a refund that hits your account before you finish reading it.

You close the app mildly annoyed. You don't know that the system ran a geospatial query eleven times, evaluated three batching candidates, increased the rider incentive twice, and held on until the food was too cold to save before it gave up.

You just know your Dosa didn't arrive.

And Zomato knows they have one chance to make that refund so fast and so clean that you open the app again tomorrow.

---

But everything we talked about until now assumes one thing. That riders exist somewhere. That the system has supply to work with, even if that supply is stubborn, attending weddings, or mid-delivery three neighbourhoods away.

The truly hard version of this problem is when supply collapses entirely.

Picture this. It is raining heavily in Bangalore. Not drizzling. The kind of rain where water is coming sideways and auto drivers are charging 3x. Everyone in the city has made the same decision simultaneously: I am not going outside, I am ordering food. Zomato's order volume spikes. And at the exact same moment, riders start logging off. Nobody wants to ride a two-wheeler in this rain. The ones who stay online are getting snapped up instantly by the first orders they see.

Your Dosa order lands in this environment.

`GEOSEARCH` at 3 km returns two riders. Both get notified. Both are already being considered for three other orders simultaneously. The radius expands to 5 km. Four riders. Same problem. Expands to 8 km. The incentive is now high enough that a rider in a dry area might consider it. Maybe one of them accepts. Maybe none of them do.

This is not a dispatch problem anymore. This is a supply problem. And no amount of elegant Redis querying or batching logic or incentive escalation fixes a supply problem in real time. You cannot algorithmically conjure riders who are not there.

This is exactly why Zomato built Active Dispatch.

Active Dispatch is a Deep Q-Network based reinforcement learning model that runs continuously in the background. It watches demand patterns, weather, time of day, historical order density by neighbourhood, and it recommends positioning to idle riders before the orders even hit. Not after. Before. It tells a rider sitting idle in Koramangala: move to Indiranagar, demand is about to spike there. The rider follows the recommendation, positions themselves in the right place, and when your order comes in they are already nearby.

The dispatch loop solves the assignment problem in real time. Active Dispatch solves the supply distribution problem before real time. These are two different systems operating at two different time horizons and keeping them architecturally separate is one of the smartest structural decisions in Zomato's entire engineering org. The real time system needs to be fast, deterministic and correct. The ML system can afford to be slower and probabilistic. Mixing their concerns would make both of them worse.

But on the truly bad days, even Active Dispatch cannot help. When an entire city decides to stay home simultaneously, when a festival empties the streets, when a weather event is severe enough that no recommendation model predicted it accurately, the supply is simply not there. Orders fail faster in this scenario because the system detects the supply collapse early and does not waste time running through the full escalation loop against an empty pool.

Your order gets cancelled. Your refund arrives. Zomato takes the loss.

And somewhere in Zomato's engineering org, an on-call alert fires. Not because one order failed. Because fifty orders failed in the same zone in the same ten minute window. A human looks at the dashboard. A supply intervention gets triggered. More riders get incentivised to come online. The storm drain under the still lake gets a little louder.

---

The next time your Zomato order gets delayed and you impatiently refresh the app, remember what is actually happening underneath that calm interface.

A geospatial query has run multiple times against a Redis instance storing the live location of hundreds of thousands of riders. An escalation loop has expanded its search radius twice and increased the payout to a stranger to convince them to ride across the city for your food. A batching algorithm has evaluated every rider currently mid-delivery to see if any of their routes can be bent in your favour. A state machine is tracking your order through every possible failure transition with a timer that knows exactly when your food stops being worth saving. An RL model trained on millions of historical orders nudged a rider into the right neighbourhood an hour ago so they would be nearby when you needed them.

And a single frontend string, "Finding a delivery partner", is holding your trust together while all of it runs.

That is what great engineering looks like. Not the part that works when everything goes right. The part that keeps working, quietly, invisibly, through every misaligned planet, every wedding, every dead battery, every monsoon, until it has genuinely exhausted every option available to it.

Zomato didn't build a food delivery app. They built a system that refuses to give up on your Dosa until the last possible moment.

I think that deserves more credit than it gets.
